Scope

What this page is, and is not

It is: the indigenous legislator elections — two nationwide constituencies, mountain indigenous and plain indigenous, across nine terms from 1995 to 2024. Every indigenous elector votes in one of them.

It is not: local public office (city and county councilors, township representatives, indigenous district chiefs). That is a different population, a different set of constituencies and a different term of office, covered on the local elections and roster pages, which are in Chinese only.

⚠ Figures on these pages cover different populations. They cannot be compared with each other, and they cannot be added.

01

Party vote share in legislative elections

Combined across both constituencies. The KMT fell from 77% in 1995 to 41% in 2024, and most of what it lost did not go to the DPP — between 2001 and 2016 it went to the People First Party and the Non-Partisan Solidarity Union, and after 2020 to independents.

This is not party identification

These are candidate vote shares, grouped by the party that nominated each candidate. Indigenous legislators are elected in multi-member nationwide constituencies: who stands, whether an incumbent is running, and how many candidates a party fields all move this number directly.

The figure closest to party leaning is the party-list vote, in section 04 — but that data covers only a small share of indigenous electors.

04

Polling stations in indigenous areas: the party-list vote

The party-list vote is not counted by ethnicity, so there is no figure for how indigenous people voted. Only one thing can be done: find the polling stations where indigenous electors are almost the entire roll, and read the party-list vote there.

Those stations are all in mountain indigenous townships, and they cover only a small share of all indigenous electors — 11.0% at the strictest threshold. Plain indigenous people living in cities are not in them at all.

Why this is not drawn as error bars

The bounds are only a few percentage points wide. Drawn as a bar with error bars they would read as a very settled number. What is unsettled is not inside the interval — it is the coverage. The interval is arithmetically certain about those stations; what is uncertain is how the remaining indigenous electors voted, and outside those stations the same arithmetic gives no useful bound at all.

The three thresholds are shown together because the trade-off between coverage and precision is what determines how far the figure generalises. Choosing one for the reader would make that decision for them.
